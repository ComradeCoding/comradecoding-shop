How to Plan a Tolerance Break With a Printable Journal

Set a length and start date, write down your reasons, plan for cravings, and keep a short daily check-in through a 14 or 28 day break, on paper.

A tolerance break is a planned pause from a habit, usually for a set number of days. Planning one on paper means deciding a few things before day 1 and then writing a little each day. The journal is a record of how the days actually went, in your own words, and something to look back on at the end.

This is a personal journal guide, not medical advice. If you have questions or concerns about your health, talk to a doctor.

Here is a simple way to plan a break and keep a journal through it, with a plain notebook or with printed pages.

Step 1: Pick a length and a start date

Choose a length you can commit to, such as 14 or 28 days. Write down the start date and work out the end date, so the finish line is fixed before day 1. Pick a start day that suits your week; some people prefer a quiet weekend, others a normal work day.

Step 2: Write your reasons

On the first page, answer a few questions in a sentence or two each:

  • Why are you taking this break?
  • What do you want to notice or learn by the end?
  • What will you do instead when the usual time comes around?
  • Who can you talk to if a day gets hard?

Keep this page where you can find it, so you can reread your own reasons on any day you want to.

Step 3: Plan for cravings

Make a short list of things you can do when a craving comes up: a walk, a shower, a call, a game, cooking something. Then plan to log them. A simple log has the date and time, what was happening, how strong it was from 1 to 5, and what you did instead. Reading the log back at the end of each week shows you which times and situations came up most.

Step 4: Keep a short daily check-in

Keep it short enough that you will actually do it every day. Five minutes is plenty:

  • Day number and date
  • Mood, energy and cravings, each from 1 to 5
  • About how many hours you slept, and how well
  • One question to answer, such as "What did I do with the time today?"
  • One thing that went well
  • Any notes

A calendar page where you cross off each day is a good visual check.

Step 5: Reflect each week, and at the end

At the end of each week, read back through the daily pages and write a short reflection: what was harder or easier than you expected, and what you want to do differently next week. A separate sleep log, one row per day with bedtime, wake time and quality, makes it easy to see the whole break at a glance.

On the last day, write a closing reflection: what you noticed, what surprised you, and what you plan to do next.

FAQ

Should I do 14 or 28 days?

That is your choice. Pick a length you can commit to and write it down before you start. The journal pages work for either.

What if I stop before the end date?

Write down what happened and when, without judging it, the same way you write any other day. The record is still useful if you plan another break later.

Do I need to print the whole journal?

No. Print what you will use. For 14 days, pages 1 to 14 and 25 to 28 are enough. Print at actual size (100%).

Is this medical advice?

No. It is a personal journal. If you have questions or concerns about your health, talk to a doctor.
